1.  Read the question and cover everything below it.

2.  Give your answer aloud in about 30 to 45 seconds, in your own words.

3.  Uncover the follow-up and scenario, and answer those too.

4.  Check yourself against the Mentor's advice.

# Stage 1: Role reality, ITSM and ticketing, and customer communication

This stage covers what the role involves, how ITSM and tickets work, and how to communicate with users.

**Approach.** User reports issue → understand → assess impact → prioritize → troubleshoot → communicate → resolve or escalate → verify → document → close

**Boundary.** Your employer sets permissions, priority labels, approvals, support hours, escalation routes, and closure rules. Serious service impact or a suspected security issue goes to the urgent escalation route promptly.

## 1.1 Role reality

### Q1. What does a Service Desk analyst actually do?

**Answer.** A Service Desk analyst helps users get back to work and handles requests for IT services. I would listen, record the issue, assess its impact, and use approved checks or support guides. The user should know what happens next, and the ticket should show it. If another team is needed, useful evidence goes with the handover. Before closure, I confirm the service works and record the outcome [[\[1\]]{.underline}](https://www.onetonline.org/link/details/15-1232.00).

**Follow-up.** *Is a Help Desk different from a Service Desk?*

Help Desk often means technical support. Service Desk can also cover service requests and communication. Titles vary, so I would check the actual duties in the job description.

**Scenario.** A caller says, \"Email is broken.\" Ask: \"What happens when you try to send or open a message, and is it stopping all your work?\" An error description tells you more than \'broken.\'

**Mentor's advice.** Show the whole support journey. Fixing a device is only one part.

### Q2. What would you handle at L1, and where would you stop?

**Answer.** At L1, I collect details, check known issues, and follow approved troubleshooting steps. Where my role allows, that includes a password reset, a standard software request, or basic device support. Identity is verified before any account action, and required approvals are checked. If a step needs access I do not have, creates wider risk, or goes beyond the guide, I stop and escalate [[\[1\]]{.underline}](https://www.onetonline.org/link/details/15-1232.00), [[\[2\]]{.underline}](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows-server/identity/ad-ds/plan/security-best-practices/implementing-least-privilege-administrative-models).

**Follow-up.** *What changes when someone moves into early L2?*

They may investigate further and hold more permissions, within limits the employer sets. Change controls and escalation still apply.

**Scenario.** An online post says to disable security software to fix an application. Do not try it. Record the symptom and request an approved next step from the responsible team.

**Mentor's advice.** Name a safe action and a clear stopping point. Avoid claiming unlimited access.

### Q3. How would you organize the start of your shift?

**Answer.** The handover and current service notices come first. Then I review assigned tickets and the shared queue for urgent impact, approaching targets, and promised updates. Tickets waiting on users or other teams still need follow-up. I take work according to the queue rules and confirm the owner of anything unclear, reassessing priorities as new information arrives [[\[1\]]{.underline}](https://www.onetonline.org/link/details/15-1232.00).

**Follow-up.** *Would you always work on the oldest ticket first?*

No. Impact, urgency, due targets, and queue rules come first. Among similar priorities, age can help decide the order.

**Scenario.** You find a routine request, an update promised for 09:00, and new reports of a site outage. Alert the incident contact promptly, then arrange coverage for the promised update.

**Mentor's advice.** Mention waiting tickets and handovers. Both are easy to overlook.

### Q4. How can you show job readiness without paid support experience?

**Answer.** I would be clear that I am a fresher, then show how I would handle a user issue, supported by a real college, lab, or volunteer example: what I observed, what I checked, how I confirmed the result, and when I needed help. If I have not used the employer\'s ticketing system, I say so and explain the workflow I understand [[\[1\]]{.underline}](https://www.onetonline.org/link/details/15-1232.00).

**Follow-up.** *Have you used ServiceNow or Jira Service Management?*

If not, say so: \"I have not used those tools yet. I understand recording, prioritizing, updating, routing, and closing tickets, and I would need training on your setup.\"

**Scenario.** An interviewer asks for a difficult-user story, and you have none. Say you have not handled that at work, then explain your approach or use a real study example, labelled as study.

**Mentor's advice.** Use \"I would\" for proposed actions and \"I did\" only for real experience.

## 1.2 ITSM and ticketing

### Q5. What is ITSM, and why do we use tickets?

**Answer.** IT service management (ITSM) is how an organization plans, delivers, and supports IT services. At the Service Desk, it means repeatable ways of handling incidents and requests. A ticket keeps the issue, owner, priority, actions, and updates in one record, so people can work together and nothing is forgotten. Phone and chat support are still recorded as required [[\[3\]]{.underline}](https://www.iso.org/standard/70636.html), [[\[4\]]{.underline}](https://www.peoplecert.org/ITIL-4), [[\[5\]]{.underline}](#ref_5).

**Follow-up.** *Is ITIL the same as ITSM?*

No. ITSM is the work of managing IT services. ITIL is guidance organizations can use for that work. A ticketing tool supports the process but is not the process.

**Scenario.** A colleague asks for a quick fix in chat. Capture the work in a ticket under the intake rules. If action is urgent, act first and complete the fields afterwards.

**Mentor's advice.** Explain what a ticket helps people do. Tool names alone do not answer the question.

### Q6. What is the difference between an incident and a service request?

**Answer.** An incident is an unplanned interruption or reduction in service quality. A service request asks for something normally provided, such as approved software, information, or access. My first question: has an existing service failed, or is something new needed? The answer decides the workflow. Some cases, such as password resets, are classified differently by employers, so their definitions apply [[\[3\]]{.underline}](https://www.iso.org/standard/70636.html), [[\[6\]]{.underline}](https://www.atlassian.com/itsm/service-request-management).

**Follow-up.** *A user cannot open a shared folder. Which type is it?*

If access that should work has stopped, it is a possible incident. If the user needs access for the first time, it is an access request.

**Scenario.** A new starter asks for a licensed application. Confirm the application and business need, then follow approval and licensing checks. Wanting it today does not make it an outage.

**Mentor's advice.** Ask whether something failed or something new is needed.

### Q7. How are an incident, a problem, and a change different?

**Answer.** An incident is the interruption or reduced quality itself. A problem is the cause, or possible cause, of one or more incidents. A change alters something that can affect a service. At L1, my aim is to restore service safely. When an issue repeats, I link the history and flag the pattern. A fix needing a controlled change goes through the change process; a ticket is not permission [[\[3\]]{.underline}](https://www.iso.org/standard/70636.html), [[\[7\]]{.underline}](https://www.atlassian.com/incident-management).

**Follow-up.** *Must you find the root cause before resolving every incident?*

No. An approved workaround can restore service, with its limits documented. Restored service does not prove the cause is gone.

**Scenario.** An application keeps failing after a temporary fix. Add earlier ticket references and failure times, and ask the responsible team to review the pattern.

**Mentor's advice.** Stay at analyst level: spot and report patterns.

### Q8. What information belongs in a useful ticket?

**Answer.** Who needs help and how to reach them, the affected service or device, and a clear symptom. Then the exact error, start time, business impact, and whether others are affected, followed by the priority reason, checks and results, status, owner, and next action. Facts and guesses stay separate. Passwords, verification codes, and unrelated personal information stay out [[\[1\]]{.underline}](https://www.onetonline.org/link/details/15-1232.00).

**Follow-up.** *What makes a good short description?*

Specific wording such as \"One user cannot print to the third-floor printer.\" \"Printer issue\" is too vague.

**Scenario.** Example note: \"10:20 local time: one user cannot print to Printer 3. Two colleagues can. No error shown. Printer 2 works as a workaround. Local queue check next.\" Add the time zone for cross-region teams.

**Mentor's advice.** Record what each check showed, so the next analyst can continue.

### Q9. How do you decide a ticket\'s priority?

**Answer.** I assess impact and urgency, then apply the employer\'s priority rules. Impact is how much business activity is affected; urgency is how soon harm becomes serious. That means asking who is affected, what work is blocked, whether a workaround exists, and whether a real deadline applies. The facts go in the ticket, and priority is reviewed when they change. Seniority or repeated calls alone do not raise it [[\[8\]]{.underline}](https://support.atlassian.com/jira-service-management-cloud/docs/what-are-priority-levels-in-jira-service-management/).

**Follow-up.** *Can an issue affecting one person still be urgent?*

Yes. That person may be doing time-critical business work. I would check the consequence and deadline rather than count users.

**Scenario.** Twenty people report a minor display issue. One user cannot submit a business-critical file before a fixed deadline. Compare workarounds and consequences before choosing the order.

**Mentor's advice.** Explain the facts behind your decision. See the example grid in the Quick reference.

### Q10. What is an SLA, and what would you do if a target is at risk?

**Answer.** A service level agreement (SLA) sets agreed service expectations, such as response and resolution targets within defined hours. A response target is not a fix deadline. I watch the applicable target and act before it is missed. If progress is blocked, I record why, escalate through the agreed route, and update the user, without promising a repair time just because a target exists [[\[3\]]{.underline}](https://www.iso.org/standard/70636.html), [[\[9\]]{.underline}](https://www.servicenow.com/docs/r/it-service-management/service-level-management/c_SLAConditions.html).

**Follow-up.** *Does putting a ticket on hold stop the SLA clock?*

Only if the configured SLA rules allow it. A status should never change just to hide a delay.

**Scenario.** A ticket nears its resolution target, and the next check needs another team\'s access. Send the evidence and flag the risk now. Tell the user when they will next hear from support.

**Mentor's advice.** Acknowledgment, updates, and resolution can each have different targets.

### Q11. What does owning a ticket mean?

**Answer.** While a ticket is assigned to me, it always has a clear next action and a responsible person. Notes and user updates stay current, dependencies are followed up, and the status is correct. A transfer follows the handover rules and names the next owner clearly. Escalation does not always move technical ownership, and sending a message is not the same as completing a handover.

**Follow-up.** *What would you leave at the end of your shift?*

Current impact, checks and results, pending action, owner, due target, and the next promised update. Urgent handovers are confirmed through the required channel.

**Scenario.** Your shift ends while a ticket waits for a vendor. Record the vendor reference, last response, open question, and next follow-up time, then confirm who tracks it next.

**Mentor's advice.** Describe ownership as visible follow-through, not personally fixing everything.

### Q12. When would you escalate, and what would you send?

**Answer.** When the next step exceeds my permissions or skills, approved checks have not helped, impact is high, or a target is at risk. Suspected security issues go to the security route promptly. The escalation carries the symptom, affected service, impact, timing, error, checks and results, and evidence, plus exactly what help is needed. The user is told when their next update is due [[\[7\]]{.underline}](https://www.atlassian.com/incident-management).

**Follow-up.** *What is functional versus hierarchical escalation?*

Functional escalation goes to a team with the right expertise or access. Hierarchical escalation goes to someone with authority over a service risk or blocker.

**Scenario.** Example: \"Four users cannot sign in to the finance application since 14:10 UTC. Web access works. Approved sign-in checks did not help. No known outage. Please check application authentication. Users expect an update at 14:40 UTC.\"

**Mentor's advice.** \"Not working, please fix\" wastes the next team\'s time.

### Q13. How would you respond to several reports of the same outage?

**Answer.** I check whether the reports share a service, location, start time, or error, and look at current notices. If the pattern suggests wider impact, the designated incident contact hears about it promptly. Related tickets are linked to the main incident, and users receive approved updates. Declaring a major incident or messaging the whole organization is only for roles whose procedure allows it [[\[7\]]{.underline}](https://www.atlassian.com/incident-management).

**Follow-up.** *Would you close the extra tickets as duplicates?*

Only under the linking and closure rules. Each affected user still needs communication and a record of impact. Linking is not closing.

**Scenario.** Six callers from different offices report the same application error within ten minutes. Capture the shared details, notify the incident contact, and give callers the agreed message without guessing the cause.

**Mentor's advice.** Show early recognition and prompt reporting.

### Q14. How do you resolve and close a ticket properly?

**Answer.** I test the original task and ask the user to confirm they can work again. The record shows the action taken, the test result, and any workaround limits. Then the employer\'s resolution and closure process applies; some systems keep a ticket resolved for a period before closing it. If the user does not reply, the contact-attempt rules apply. Silence alone does not prove a fix worked [[\[10\]]{.underline}](https://www.servicenow.com/docs/r/it-service-management/incident-management/resolve-and-close-an-incident.html).

**Follow-up.** *What if the issue returns after closure?*

I would capture the new impact and timing, reopen or create a linked ticket as the workflow requires, and review earlier actions.

**Scenario.** A test page prints, but the user\'s original document still fails. The task is not restored. Continue within scope or escalate with that result.

**Mentor's advice.** Verify the user\'s task, and keep resolution and closure distinct.

## 1.3 Customer communication

### Q15. How would you begin a support conversation?

**Answer.** I introduce myself and let the user explain before narrowing things down. A useful opener is \"What were you trying to do, and what happened instead?\" Next, I confirm the affected service, start time, scope, and work impact, and repeat the key point back in plain words. Identity checks come before any account action or remote access. One clear question at a time.

**Follow-up.** *How do you avoid making the user repeat everything?*

I read the existing ticket first, summarize what is known, and ask only about gaps or changes.

**Scenario.** The user says, \"Nothing works.\" Ask: \"Which task are you trying to finish right now?\" Then ask what they see. A broad complaint becomes something you can investigate.

**Mentor's advice.** Start open, then narrow the questions.

### Q16. How would you help a user who is not technical?

**Answer.** I use everyday words and one step at a time, explaining why a step matters when that helps. After each step, I ask what the user sees rather than assume it worked. Before a restart or other action that interrupts work, I check for unsaved work and agree on timing. Remote help uses the approved tool and consent process, and the user enters their own passwords.

**Follow-up.** *What if the user still cannot follow the steps?*

I would rephrase or use an approved visual guide, then arrange remote or in-person help if needed, without blaming the user.

**Scenario.** Instead of \"Terminate the process,\" say: \"This will close the application. Is any work still unsaved?\" If they are unsure, pause and check recovery options first.

**Mentor's advice.** \"Do you understand?\" often hides confusion. Ask what happened instead.

### Q17. How would you deal with an angry user?

**Answer.** I let them explain the impact and keep my tone steady, for example: \"I understand this is stopping your work. Let me confirm what is affected so I can take the right next step.\" I avoid arguing, blaming another team, or promising an instant fix. Instead, I explain what I can do now and when I will update them. Abusive or threatening calls follow the workplace procedure.

**Follow-up.** *What if they demand your manager?*

I would follow the complaint or escalation process with a factual summary, while the service issue keeps its owner and next action.

**Scenario.** A caller says, \"You people never fix anything.\" Reply: \"You have had to contact us again. I will review the earlier ticket so we do not repeat steps.\" Then confirm the current symptom.

**Mentor's advice.** Acknowledge the specific disruption. Avoid \"calm down.\"

### Q18. How do you set expectations when you do not know the fix time?

**Answer.** I say what is known, what is still being checked, and what happens next, then give a realistic update time through the agreed channel: \"The application team is investigating. There is no confirmed repair time yet. I will update you by 15:00, even if nothing changes.\" That time must fit support coverage. Any verified workaround comes with its limits explained.

**Follow-up.** *What if you miss an update you promised?*

Contact the user promptly, acknowledge it, give current facts, set a new time, and fix the gap that caused it.

**Scenario.** The resolver team has nothing new at update time. Send a factual update anyway: \"Investigation continues. No repair time is confirmed. The workaround remains available. Next update at 16:00.\"

**Mentor's advice.** Promise only updates you can arrange.

### Q19. What if a user asks you to bypass a support rule?

**Answer.** I clarify the business need and explain the safe route. Access without approval is not possible until the approval is recorded, so I help the user reach the right approver or the documented urgent request process. Sharing credentials, skipping identity checks, or granting extra access under pressure is not an option. Genuine urgency goes to escalation for an authorized decision [[\[2\]]{.underline}](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows-server/identity/ad-ds/plan/security-best-practices/implementing-least-privilege-administrative-models).

**Follow-up.** *What if their manager says it is fine?*

I would check that the manager is an authorized approver and the approval is recorded. A verbal okay does not replace the process.

**Scenario.** A caller claims to be an executive and demands a reset without verification. Use the approved verification route. If they cannot complete it, escalate and leave the account unchanged.

**Mentor's advice.** Offer the next safe step so the request can still move forward.

### Q20. What would you do if you gave a user the wrong instruction?

**Answer.** I acknowledge it promptly and stop anything that could make it worse. Next, I check what the user did and whether work or data was affected. If I can correct it safely within my role, I do; otherwise, I escalate with the facts. The ticket gets an accurate update, the user hears the next step, and an incident caused by the mistake goes through the reporting process.

**Follow-up.** *How would you explain it without losing the user\'s trust?*

Directly: \"The step I gave you was not correct here. Please pause while I check the safe next action.\" Then follow through.

**Scenario.** You asked for a restart before checking for unsaved work. Pause changes, find out what was lost, and seek approved recovery help. Do not promise recovery or hide the instruction.

**Mentor's advice.** Show honesty, an impact check, and recovery.

# Stage 2: Troubleshooting and endpoint support

This stage covers a repeatable troubleshooting method, Windows and macOS support, printers and peripherals, and managed devices.

**Approach.** Understand the task → assess impact → check known issues → narrow the cause → choose a safe check → apply an approved fix or escalate → verify the original task → document

**Boundary.** Tool access does not grant permission for every action. Repairs, recovery keys, service restarts, drivers, updates, and management changes follow the approved route. Advanced repair and fleet administration belong to specialist teams.

## 2.1 Troubleshooting method

### Q21. Walk me through your troubleshooting method.

**Answer.** First, I confirm what the user was trying to do, what happened, and how work is affected. Then I check known issues and recent changes, form a likely explanation, and choose a safe check to test it. Each result is recorded before the next change. If a fix is within my permissions, I apply it and test the original task. At my limit, I send the evidence to the right team.

**Follow-up.** *Why change only one thing at a time?*

It connects the result to the action. Several changes together may restore service without showing which one helped, or may cause a new fault.

**Scenario.** A laptop cannot use an external display. Before changing drivers, check the display\'s power, input, and cable, one part at a time. If a new cable restores the picture, record it and test the user\'s normal setup.

**Mentor's advice.** For each check, say what its result would tell you.

### Q22. How would you narrow down whether a fault is local or widespread?

**Answer.** I compare what works with what fails: one application, the whole device, one user, or several users. Where the procedure allows, I test another approved file, application, device, or connection, changing one factor at a time. The result narrows the area but does not prove the cause. Before making local changes, I check service notices, because local fixes will not help a wider outage.

**Follow-up.** *Would you ask someone else for their password to test?*

No. An authorized user can sign in themselves if the test is allowed. Otherwise, I would use an approved test account where the procedure permits it.

**Scenario.** One document fails to open, but a new blank document opens in the same application. Investigate that file or its access next, rather than reinstalling the application or replacing the laptop.

**Mentor's advice.** Use comparisons to narrow the fault. \"It worked elsewhere\" is a clue, not a diagnosis.

### Q23. What would you do if you could not reproduce an intermittent issue?

**Answer.** I would ask for the exact time, frequency, steps, and conditions, and check whether it follows sleep, docking, a particular file, or another repeatable event. An approved screenshot or relevant logs from that time help, without unnecessary personal data. I record that I could not reproduce it and agree on what to capture next time. Serious impact or a pattern needing deeper analysis goes to escalation.

**Follow-up.** *Would you close it because it works during your call?*

Not for that reason alone. I would record the successful test and the remaining uncertainty, then follow the team\'s closure rules.

**Scenario.** A screen goes blank only after the laptop wakes on its dock. Ask whether the built-in display still works, note the dock model and wake time, and arrange an approved repeat test.

**Mentor's advice.** Record the conditions around the failure. \"Could not reproduce\" still needs a next step.

### Q24. How would you use Event Viewer without guessing the cause?

**Answer.** I start from the reported failure time and the affected component, then review Application or System events near that time. For each relevant entry, I record the source, event ID, time, and message, and compare them with what the user saw. An error entry is a clue, not proof. An approved guide helps me interpret it, and only relevant evidence goes to another team [[\[11\]]{.underline}](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/experience/system-configuration-tools-in-windows).

**Follow-up.** *What is the difference between Task Manager and Event Viewer?*

Task Manager shows current processes and resource use. Event Viewer shows recorded events. The first suits a live slowdown; the second suits investigating a past crash.

**Scenario.** A crash happened at 11:42, but the first red error is from yesterday. Match the time and source before using any entry. An event ID alone does not identify a fix.

**Mentor's advice.** Keep logs intact. Do not clear them or treat every warning as a fault.

## 2.2 Windows support

### Q25. A user says their Windows PC is slow. What would you check?

**Answer.** I would ask whether the whole PC or one task is slow, and when it started. During the slowdown, Task Manager shows CPU, memory, and disk activity. I also check free storage, pending restarts, and recent changes, and compare the activity with the user\'s work instead of treating one high reading as a fault. Then I try a small approved action, such as closing an unused application after saving work, and retest [[\[11\]]{.underline}](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/experience/system-configuration-tools-in-windows), [[\[12\]]{.underline}](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/experience/performance-optimization/tips-to-improve-pc-performance-in-windows).

**Follow-up.** *Does high memory use mean the PC needs more RAM?*

Not by itself. I would check whether the slowdown matches sustained memory pressure. Memory is working space; drive space is storage. Evidence comes before any upgrade.

**Scenario.** Disk activity rises during an approved update and settles when it finishes. Record whether the slowdown ends too. Do not disable the update service to lower the reading.

**Mentor's advice.** Do not end an unfamiliar process just because it is busy.

### Q26. How would you handle an application that freezes or crashes?

**Answer.** I check whether only that application is affected and what action triggers the failure, then capture the error and version and look for known issues. If the app still responds, I save work and close it normally. If ending it is necessary, I explain the risk to unsaved work and follow the approved procedure. After reopening, I test the same task. Repeated failures need evidence, not repeated forced closes.

**Follow-up.** *Would reinstalling the application be your first fix?*

No. Reinstalling can affect settings, local data, licensing, or add-ins. I would check scope and approved repair options first, and confirm authorization.

**Scenario.** A drawing application crashes only when exporting one project. Test a permitted sample project and capture the error. Do not overwrite the original or assume the installation is damaged.

**Mentor's advice.** Separate a freeze from a crash, and protect unsaved work first.

### Q27. What is a Windows service, and when would you restart one?

**Answer.** A Windows service is a background program supporting a system or application function. I check its status in the Services console against the support guide; some services run only when needed, so stopped is not always faulty. A restart needs permission and a procedure that calls for it, plus a check of dependencies and who could be affected. Afterwards, I test the function and record the result [[\[11\]]{.underline}](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/experience/system-configuration-tools-in-windows).

**Follow-up.** *What if the service stops again straight away?*

I would capture the error and event details, then escalate if the guide offers no safe next step. Repeated restarts hide the pattern and interrupt work.

**Scenario.** A guide suggests restarting the local Print Spooler. Confirm you are on the user\'s device, understand the effect on local jobs, and check authorization. A print server restart affects many users.

**Mentor's advice.** Checking a service and changing it are different actions.

### Q28. How would you handle a failed or pending Windows update?

**Answer.** I record the update identifier, the error code, and whether it is downloading, installing, or waiting to restart. Power, connectivity, storage, and the employer\'s update guidance come next. For an approved restart, the user saves work and we agree on timing. I avoid interrupting an active installation. If it keeps failing, I escalate to endpoint support instead of retrying or changing update policies [[\[13\]]{.underline}](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/deployment/updates-lifecycle/troubleshoot-problems-updating-windows).

**Follow-up.** *Would you immediately remove an update after a new fault?*

No. Timing suggests a link but does not prove one. I would check known issues and gather evidence. Removal follows the approved process.

**Scenario.** A user says an update is \"stuck,\" but progress is still changing. Check the elapsed time against official guidance. Do not force a power-off because it is slower than expected.

**Mentor's advice.** A managed device is not a personal test machine.

### Q29. A laptop will not start properly. What do you check first?

**Answer.** I clarify whether there is no power, no picture, or a failure while Windows starts. Power lights, the charger, the display, and any exact message all matter. Approved external checks help, such as a compatible known-working charger or display. If Windows shows a stop code, I record it with recent changes. Recovery steps that could affect data, encryption, or settings go to escalation [[\[13\]]{.underline}](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/deployment/updates-lifecycle/troubleshoot-problems-updating-windows), [[\[14\]]{.underline}](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/hardware/display-graphics/troubleshooting-blank-screens-in-windows).

**Follow-up.** *What if the screen asks for a BitLocker recovery key?*

I would follow the approved identity and recovery-key process. Encryption is never bypassed, and the key never goes into a chat or ticket.

**Scenario.** The laptop has power and an external monitor works, but the built-in display stays blank. That points to the display path. Record the comparison instead of reporting that Windows will not start.

**Mentor's advice.** Distinguish power, display, and startup faults before proposing a repair.

### Q30. How would you help a user whose system drive is nearly full?

**Answer.** I check which drive is full and review storage categories before deleting anything. Approved cleanup options are used only after reviewing what they remove, and needed files must be backed up or safely stored first. System folders, user profiles, and unfamiliar application data stay untouched. Afterwards, I check free space and retest the failed task, such as saving a file or installing an update [[\[15\]]{.underline}](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/experience/storage-filemanagement/manage-drive-space-with-storage-sense).

**Follow-up.** *Is deleting a cloud-synced file a safe way to free local space?*

Not necessarily. It may delete the file from the synced service too. Removing only the local copy is a different, supported option.

**Scenario.** Downloads holds large files, and the user does not know which are needed. Review them with the user or the owner. Do not clean the whole folder because it is large.

**Mentor's advice.** Keeping needed data matters more than free space.

## 2.3 Printers and peripherals

### Q31. A user cannot print. How would you narrow down the issue?

**Answer.** I confirm the intended printer and whether one document, one application, or all printing fails. Then I check power, paper, any display error, the connection, and the selected printer and queue. Where permitted, a simple test document, another approved printer, or another user\'s result separates a document, device, printer, or shared-service issue. After a fix, I test the original document [[\[16\]]{.underline}](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/hardware/printer/fix-printer-connection-and-printing-problems-in-windows).

**Follow-up.** *If a test page prints, is the issue resolved?*

Not always. The test shows one printing path works. The user\'s original document and application may still fail.

**Scenario.** Several users cannot print to one shared printer, but other printers work. Check that printer and its shared path, and report the common impact before touching anyone\'s driver.

**Mentor's advice.** Start with scope and the selected printer.

### Q32. What would you do with a print job stuck in the queue?

**Answer.** I check the printer status and whether the queue is local or shared, then confirm with the user that the affected job can be canceled and resent. If that does not clear it, I follow the printing guide. Restarting a service or clearing queue files may need elevated permission and can affect other jobs, so a shared queue is never cleared without authorization. Finally, the user resends once [[\[16\]]{.underline}](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/hardware/printer/fix-printer-connection-and-printing-problems-in-windows).

**Follow-up.** *Why not keep pressing Print until something appears?*

Duplicate jobs may all print when the fault clears. Existing jobs get checked first, then one controlled retry.

**Scenario.** One large job is blocking a shared queue. Record its owner, time, and affected users. If you lack permission, ask print support to act rather than canceling other people\'s work.

**Mentor's advice.** State whose jobs your action could affect.

### Q33. How would you troubleshoot a USB device, dock, or external monitor?

**Answer.** I identify the exact device, connection, and symptom, then check power, cables, ports, and the monitor\'s input. If allowed, I try a compatible known-working cable or port, or connect directly to the laptop to test whether the dock is involved. Device Manager shows recognition or errors. Only approved drivers and firmware are used, and I change one device or driver at a time [[\[17\]]{.underline}](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/hardware/display-graphics/troubleshoot-external-monitor-connections-in-windows).

**Follow-up.** *Does every USB-C cable support an external display?*

No. The cable, port, and dock must all support video. A cable that charges a laptop may not carry a display signal.

**Scenario.** A monitor works directly from the laptop but fails through a dock. Compare the dock\'s power, ports, and cabling. This narrows the fault to that path without proving the dock is broken.

**Mentor's advice.** A comparison only helps when the replacement is compatible.

### Q34. How would you check a microphone or camera that does not work?

**Answer.** First come the selected device, the physical mute switch or camera cover, and the connection. Then the application\'s device selection and operating system permissions, plus site permission in a browser. An approved recording or camera test separates an application issue from a device problem. Managed settings and driver changes follow the support process, not a workaround [[\[18\]]{.underline}](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/privacy/windows-camera-microphone-and-privacy).

**Follow-up.** *What if the device works in one application but not another?*

I would check the failing application\'s device selection and permissions. The working test shows the device functions, though it does not rule out every compatibility issue.

**Scenario.** A headset microphone is detected, but the meeting app uses the laptop microphone. Select the right input with the user and run an approved test, not a recording of a real meeting.

**Mentor's advice.** Check selection, mute, and permissions before replacing hardware.

## 2.4 macOS and managed devices

### Q35. What macOS basics would you check before supporting a Mac?

**Answer.** I confirm the model, chip, and macOS version in About This Mac. Finder handles files, System Settings handles device settings, and Activity Monitor shows processes and resources. As on Windows, I begin with the symptom and a safe comparison. Shortcuts, permissions, and recovery steps differ by model and version, so I use the matching guide and the organization\'s procedures on a managed Mac [[\[19\]]{.underline}](https://support.apple.com/guide/mac-help/get-system-information-about-your-mac-syspr35536/mac), [[\[20\]]{.underline}](https://support.apple.com/en-gb/102445).

**Follow-up.** *How would you handle an app blocked by a security warning?*

I would capture the message, confirm the app\'s approved source, and use the software request route. The warning is never overridden and management profiles are never removed.

**Scenario.** A colleague suggests an Intel Mac reset procedure for a Mac with Apple silicon. Check the model and current Apple guidance first. Similar symptoms do not mean the same procedure applies.

**Mentor's advice.** Show you can find system details and use the right guide. Do not invent Mac experience.

### Q36. A Mac is slow or an app is not responding. What would you do?

**Answer.** I check whether one app or the whole Mac is affected and what the user was doing. Activity Monitor shows CPU use and memory pressure during the problem, and I check storage and recent changes too. An app that can quit normally is closed after saving work. If Force Quit is necessary, I explain the risk to unsaved changes first. Then I reopen, retest, and investigate repeated failures [[\[21\]]{.underline}](https://support.apple.com/en-ie/guide/activity-monitor/actmntr1002/mac), [[\[22\]]{.underline}](https://support.apple.com/en-gb/guide/activity-monitor/actmntr1004/mac).

**Follow-up.** *Does a large amount of used memory prove a Mac is short of RAM?*

No. The memory pressure graph and the actual slowdown matter more than used memory alone.

**Scenario.** A user sees the spinning wait cursor during a large export, but progress continues. Check that it is still working before forcing it to quit. Record any repeated hang on the same file.

**Mentor's advice.** Force Quit can lose work and does not explain the cause.

### Q37. What does L1 support do with a managed device or company app portal?

**Answer.** A managed device receives organization settings, approved apps, and security requirements through a management service. At L1, I confirm the correct device record and, if my role permits, review its status, last check-in, and any app error. I might guide an approved portal sync. Missing apps and policy changes go to endpoint support. Enrollment and security requirements are never removed to clear a message [[\[23\]]{.underline}](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/intune/user-help/device-actions/sync-device-windows).

**Follow-up.** *Does a portal sync guarantee that an app will appear immediately?*

No. A sync starts a check-in, but availability depends on assignment, eligibility, licensing, and processing time.

**Scenario.** An approved app is missing from Company Portal. Confirm the work account, device, connection, and portal status, and record the sync result. Ask the responsible team to check, instead of downloading an unapproved installer.

**Mentor's advice.** Wipe, retire, and unenroll are not routine troubleshooting steps.

### Q38. After a Windows update, one user\'s app keeps crashing. What would you do?

**Answer.** I confirm the app, update, crash time, impact, and any unsaved work at risk. After checking known issues, I test the same task with an approved sample file and ask whether others are affected. The error, app and Windows versions, and relevant events all get captured. A safe restart or approved repair may be tested if the guide allows. If crashes continue, I escalate with the results, without assuming the update caused it [[\[13\]]{.underline}](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/deployment/updates-lifecycle/troubleshoot-problems-updating-windows).

**Follow-up.** *What would useful escalation evidence sound like?*

\"One laptop crashes when exporting from the design app. A sample file also fails. An approved restart did not help. Versions, update identifier, crash time, and event details are attached.\"

**Scenario.** The user offers to let you disable antivirus or uninstall the update. Explain that both need authorization. If no safe action remains, send the evidence and offer an approved workaround.

**Mentor's advice.** Timing alone does not prove the update caused the crash.

# Stage 3: Identity, access, networking, and VPN

This stage covers identity systems, account recovery, access requests, network foundations, diagnostic commands, and VPN support.

**Approach.** Confirm the user and task → assess impact → identify the failed step → check evidence → take an authorized action or escalate → verify access → document

**Boundary.** Verify identity before any account recovery or access change. Keep passwords, recovery secrets, and verification codes out of tickets. Changes to authentication policies, DNS, routing, and shared infrastructure go to the responsible team.

## 3.1 Identity and access

### Q39. What are Active Directory, Microsoft Entra ID, and Okta?

**Answer.** All three manage identities and access, but they are different systems. Active Directory Domain Services commonly supports Windows domain accounts, computers, and policies. Microsoft Entra ID is a cloud identity service used with Microsoft 365 and other applications. Okta is another identity platform for sign-on and authentication. At L1, I identify which system manages the account and application before acting, since connected systems may not update instantly [[\[24\]]{.underline}](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/entra/fundamentals/compare).

**Follow-up.** *Is Microsoft Entra ID just a new name for Active Directory?*

It is the new name for Azure Active Directory, not for Active Directory Domain Services. I would use the correct name and check the employer\'s setup.

**Scenario.** A user can sign in to their Windows laptop but not a cloud application. Confirm which identity service that application uses. A laptop sign-in does not prove every identity service works.

**Mentor's advice.** Explain each system\'s purpose. Do not claim they share one console or password behavior.

### Q40. What is the difference between authentication and authorization?

**Answer.** Authentication checks who is signing in. Authorization decides what that identity can access or do. A user can sign in successfully and still lack permission for a folder or application. So I ask where the failure occurs and capture the exact message. A sign-in failure points to the account and authentication process. A denial after sign-in points to the access request and permission route, not a password reset [[\[25\]]{.underline}](https://developer.okta.com/docs/concepts/sso-overview/).

**Follow-up.** *Where does single sign-on fit?*

Single sign-on (SSO) lets one session reach several integrated applications. Each still needs the right permissions, and policy can require another step. SSO does not mean access to everything.

**Scenario.** An employee signs in to the company portal but sees no tile for an application. Check whether it is assigned to them. Password resets will not create a missing assignment.

**Mentor's advice.** Locate the failure: proving identity, meeting a sign-in requirement, or holding permission.

### Q41. How would you reset a user\'s password safely?

**Answer.** I verify identity through the approved process and confirm the exact account and service, then check whether self-service recovery is available. If authorized to reset it, I use the correct identity system and the secure handover procedure for any temporary credential. The user sets their own new password. I verify sign-in and record the action without storing passwords or codes. Failed verification goes to the recovery escalation route [[\[26\]]{.underline}](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/entra/fundamentals/users-reset-password-azure-portal).

**Follow-up.** *Would you reset a synchronized account in any available console?*

No. The system that controls the password, synchronization, and any writeback settings decides where a reset is supported and takes effect. I would use the designated route.

**Scenario.** A caller gives an employee\'s name and department and asks for a temporary password at a new personal address. Those details do not verify identity. Use the approved verification and delivery route.

**Mentor's advice.** Knowing an employee\'s details is not proof of owning the account.

### Q42. What would you do with an account that keeps locking out?

**Answer.** I confirm the account, identity system, exact message, and lockout timing. After verifying identity, I check the account state within my permissions. An unlock and a password reset are different actions, so the guide for the actual condition applies. If it locks again, I ask about recent password changes and devices or apps with saved credentials, then escalate with evidence if needed. I never weaken the lockout policy [[\[27\]]{.underline}](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/troubleshoot/windows-server/user-profiles-and-logon/cached-domain-logon-information).

**Follow-up.** *Could a user still sign in to a laptop while offline?*

In some domain setups, cached sign-in information allows offline access. That does not prove the current password or account state is valid.

**Scenario.** The account locks again whenever an older phone reconnects. Saved credentials are worth checking. Confirm the pattern and use the approved procedure, without assuming the phone is the only cause.

**Mentor's advice.** Treat repeated lockouts as a pattern. Report unfamiliar sign-in activity through the security route.

### Q43. What is MFA, and how would you troubleshoot a failed MFA step?

**Answer.** Multifactor authentication (MFA) uses more than one type of evidence to verify identity, such as a password and an approved authenticator. First, I confirm the user started the sign-in and identify the failing method. Then I check the account, device connectivity, and exact message; for time-based codes, device time may matter. An already registered alternative may be used if allowed. Recovery follows the verified process. MFA is never disabled [[\[28\]]{.underline}](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/entra/identity/authentication/concept-authentication-methods-manage).

**Follow-up.** *Do two passwords count as two authentication factors?*

No. Both are something the user knows. MFA combines different factor types, and some passwordless methods also meet MFA requirements.

**Scenario.** A user waits for a push notification, but the sign-in page asks for an app code. Confirm the method shown and guide the user to enter the code themselves, never reading it to you.

**Mentor's advice.** Never approve a prompt or collect a one-time code for the user.

### Q44. A user has lost their MFA phone. How would you restore access?

**Answer.** I use the approved identity verification and account recovery process, and check whether the user has another registered method they may use. Removing a lost method or registering a new one is done only within my role, or routed to identity support. The lost device is reported through the required process. A new phone number is never added just because the caller asks urgently [[\[28\]]{.underline}](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/entra/identity/authentication/concept-authentication-methods-manage), [[\[29\]]{.underline}](https://sec.okta.com/articles/2025/12/account-recovery-without-password-resets/).

**Follow-up.** *What if the user receives MFA prompts they did not start?*

They should not approve them and should report it through the security route. If one was already approved, I record that and escalate promptly.

**Scenario.** A caller says their phone is lost and wants a password reset and a new MFA method. Treat it as full account recovery. A password reset alone does not permit replacing the second factor.

**Mentor's advice.** Recovery changes who can prove ownership. Keep the identity check strong under pressure.

### Q45. How would you handle a request for folder or application access?

**Answer.** I confirm the exact resource, access level, business need, and authorized approver, and check the approved request before any change. If my role allows it, I use the standard group or application assignment for that access. Then I verify the result with the user and record what changed. A new permission design or wider access than approved goes to the resource owner or identity team [[\[30\]]{.underline}](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/entra/fundamentals/concept-learn-about-groups).

**Follow-up.** *Why use a standard access group instead of copying a colleague\'s permissions?*

Standard groups are easier to review. A colleague may hold extra or historical permissions. I would use the approved role and check any end date for temporary access.

**Scenario.** A user wants edit rights because a colleague has them, but the approval covers read-only access. Apply only the approved level, or ask for clarification.

**Mentor's advice.** The requester and the authorized approver may be different people.

### Q46. What would you do during new-starter onboarding?

**Answer.** I work from an authorized onboarding request with the person\'s details, start date, role, and access. After checking for an existing account, I follow the approved naming and provisioning process. Depending on my permissions, that may include a managed device, standard apps, and group assignments. The first sign-in and MFA setup use the secure process, and I test the work tasks with the user. Unfinished items are handed over clearly [[\[30\]]{.underline}](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/entra/fundamentals/concept-learn-about-groups).

**Follow-up.** *What if the person starts today but an approval is missing?*

I would flag the impact to the approver through the urgent request process and continue approved tasks, without granting unapproved access.

**Scenario.** A manager emails an application list for a new starter. Confirm it uses the required authorization route. Do not create a second account because the first is not visible in one tool.

**Mentor's advice.** Show preparation, approval, and a working first sign-in. Not every onboarding task belongs to L1.

### Q47. How would you handle an employee\'s offboarding request?

**Answer.** I act only on an authorized request and confirm the identity, effective time, and required tasks. The offboarding checklist covers access removal, session handling, device return, and any approved data handover. I complete the steps assigned to my role and track the rest with the responsible teams, recording completion and exceptions promptly. Accounts and mailboxes are never deleted as a shortcut, because retention needs separate approval [[\[31\]]{.underline}](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/entra/identity/users/users-revoke-access).

**Follow-up.** *Does disabling an account immediately end every application session?*

Not necessarily. Existing sessions and app-specific access can need separate action, so I would complete the full checklist.

**Scenario.** A departure takes effect at 17:00 in another region. Confirm the time zone and authorized timing before scheduling. For urgent action, use the escalation route and record who owns it.

**Mentor's advice.** Offboarding is time-sensitive. Do not invent retention rules.

### Q48. A user signs in but gets an access denied message. What would you check?

**Answer.** I capture the resource, account, time, and exact message, and confirm whether access worked before or is new. Then I check the approved assignment and any status my role can see. A denial can come from missing permissions, a device requirement, or a sign-in policy, so the wording alone does not reveal the cause. Policy checks go to the responsible team with the error details, and the policy stays unchanged [[\[32\]]{.underline}](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/entra/identity/conditional-access/troubleshoot-conditional-access).

**Follow-up.** *What does Conditional Access mean at L1?*

It applies sign-in requirements based on conditions such as user, application, or device. At L1, I capture the failure details, and no exception is added just to remove a block.

**Scenario.** A user opens an application on their managed laptop but is blocked on a personal device. Check the device requirement. Access on one device does not authorize bypassing the rule on another.

**Mentor's advice.** A password reset is not a cure for access denied.

## 3.2 Network foundations

### Q49. What do an IP address, subnet, and default gateway do?

**Answer.** An IP address identifies a network interface. The subnet mask or prefix shows which addresses are on the local network. The default gateway routes traffic to other networks when no more specific route applies. I check these on the active connection against the approved setup. A device may have several interfaces and both IPv4 and IPv6 addresses, so the first address shown is not necessarily the relevant one [[\[33\]]{.underline}](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows-server/networking/dns/queries-lookups).

**Follow-up.** *Is a private IP address automatically wrong for internet access?*

No. Private IPv4 addresses are normal inside many networks, and routers commonly translate them for internet access.

**Scenario.** A laptop reaches a local device but not a service on another network. Check the gateway or routing path, destination availability, and access rules. Local success does not prove the wider path works.

**Mentor's advice.** Explain each setting\'s job. Never assign addresses without the approved configuration.

### Q50. What is DNS, and how can it affect a user\'s application?

**Answer.** The Domain Name System (DNS) resolves names to information such as IP addresses. A user can have a working connection but still fail to reach an application whose name does not resolve correctly. I confirm the exact name and check the DNS path with an approved test. Internal names may need company DNS or a VPN. I compare the result with the symptom instead of changing DNS servers as a general fix [[\[33\]]{.underline}](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows-server/networking/dns/queries-lookups).

**Follow-up.** *Would opening a website directly by IP always prove whether DNS is the problem?*

No. Many websites need the host name for routing or certificate checks. An approved name-resolution test is more reliable.

**Scenario.** Public websites open, but an internal application name does not resolve offsite. Check the required company connection. A public DNS server may not know internal names.

**Mentor's advice.** DNS can fail while basic connectivity still works.

### Q51. What is DHCP, and what would a 169.254 address tell you?

**Answer.** Dynamic Host Configuration Protocol (DHCP) gives devices an IP address and other settings automatically. On a Windows connection expected to use DHCP, an automatically assigned IPv4 address beginning with 169.254 can indicate no valid lease was obtained. It does not prove the DHCP server is down. I check the active adapter, the connection, and whether others are affected, then send evidence to network support if the configuration is still missing [[\[34\]]{.underline}](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows-server/troubleshoot/troubleshoot-dhcp-issue).

**Follow-up.** *Can a device with that address communicate at all?*

It may reach compatible devices on the same local link, but not normal routed internet access. Other interfaces or IPv6 may still work.

**Scenario.** One wired laptop has a link-local address while nearby devices work. Check its cable, port, and adapter. Do not copy a colleague\'s address, which creates a duplicate and another outage.

**Mentor's advice.** A link-local address is a clue, not a reason to restart a shared DHCP server.

### Q52. A user says the internet is down. How would you check it?

**Answer.** I ask whether one application or all online work fails, and whether others are affected. Then I check the active wired or wireless connection and its IP settings. Approved local, external, and name-resolution tests narrow the failure, along with known service issues and any required proxy or VPN. Those results decide the next safe check. The network stack is never reset before the scope is understood [[\[35\]]{.underline}](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/experience/connectivity-networking/fix-wi-fi-connection-issues-in-windows).

**Follow-up.** *Does a connected Wi-Fi icon prove internet access works?*

No. It shows a wireless connection, not working DNS, routing, or a working destination service.

**Scenario.** A public website opens, but one business application fails for several users. Check that application\'s status. Do not report an internet outage or change adapter settings for everyone.

**Mentor's advice.** Test the path in stages. Before disconnecting a remote user, plan the reconnection.

### Q53. How would you troubleshoot weak or unreliable Wi-Fi?

**Answer.** I confirm the intended network, location, signal, and when drops occur, and check whether nearby users are affected or an approved wired connection works. Patterns related to movement, distance, or a recent device change are useful. If the guide allows, I try a controlled reconnect or another approved location and record the results before escalating. Access points and managed wireless profiles are left alone without authorization [[\[35\]]{.underline}](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/experience/connectivity-networking/fix-wi-fi-connection-issues-in-windows).

**Follow-up.** *What if the user is connected to a guest network?*

Guest access may allow internet but block internal resources. I would confirm the correct network and never share someone else\'s credentials.

**Scenario.** Three people lose connectivity in one meeting room, but their laptops work elsewhere. Capture the location, times, network name, and devices for network support, instead of reinstalling drivers.

**Mentor's advice.** Signal, network admission, and internet access can each fail separately.

### Q54. What would you look for in ipconfig output?

**Answer.** On Windows, ipconfig shows address information for network adapters, and ipconfig /all adds details such as DHCP status and DNS servers. I first identify the active adapter, then check its address, subnet, gateway, and settings against the expected configuration. Virtual adapters also appear, so an unrelated entry should not drive the diagnosis. Only necessary details go into the ticket [[\[36\]]{.underline}](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows-server/administration/windows-commands/ipconfig).

**Follow-up.** *Does \"Media disconnected\" mean the entire computer is offline?*

No. It describes that one adapter. An unused Ethernet adapter can show disconnected while Wi-Fi works.

**Scenario.** You are helping a remote user over their only connection. Do not run ipconfig /release, which can end the session. Any renewal needs an approved reason and a reconnection plan.

**Mentor's advice.** Reading configuration and changing it are different actions.

## 3.3 Diagnostic tools and VPN

### Q55. What does ping test, and what does it not prove?

**Answer.** Ping sends ICMP echo requests and reports replies and their timing. I use it with an approved target when the guide calls for a reachability test. A reply shows this traffic reached the target and returned at that moment. It does not prove an application works. A timeout may mean the target or path is unavailable, but filtering can also block replies, so I compare with other permitted checks [[\[37\]]{.underline}](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows-server/administration/windows-commands/ping).

**Follow-up.** *Does a successful ping prove that a secure website is available?*

No. Ping uses ICMP and does not test a web connection such as HTTPS on TCP port 443. The host can reply while the web service fails.

**Scenario.** A server does not reply to ping, but its web application opens. Record both results. The organization may block echo traffic, so a timeout alone does not mean the server is down.

**Mentor's advice.** Say what the test proves and what remains unknown.

### Q56. How would you use nslookup during troubleshooting?

**Answer.** I run nslookup with the affected host name and read the server and answer shown, comparing the result with the expected service. A timeout means no answer arrived in time. Name-not-found is different: a DNS server replied that the name does not exist in its view. I check spelling and the intended DNS server, then escalate with the result if needed. A successful lookup does not prove application access [[\[38\]]{.underline}](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows-server/administration/windows-commands/nslookup).

**Follow-up.** *If nslookup succeeds but the browser fails, is DNS fully ruled out?*

No. The browser may use a cache or a different resolver path. The lookup only shows what that server returned.

**Scenario.** An internal name returns name-not-found from a public resolver. Confirm the required company DNS or VPN path. Do not send internal names to other public resolvers.

**Mentor's advice.** Record the queried name, server, answer, and time.

### Q57. What is a VPN, and how would you handle a connection failure?

**Answer.** A virtual private network (VPN) creates a protected connection to company resources over another network. I first check that the underlying network works, then confirm the approved client, profile, exact error, and failure point. Depending on the message, sign-in, MFA, device requirements, or known service issues come next. The client version and failure time go into any escalation. Security controls stay on, and no other VPN is installed [[\[39\]]{.underline}](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/experience/connectivity-networking/connect-to-a-vpn-in-windows).

**Follow-up.** *Does a company VPN always carry all internet traffic?*

No. Some configurations carry all traffic and others carry selected traffic. The organization decides, and routes are not changed during routine L1 support.

**Scenario.** The VPN fails on hotel Wi-Fi that has not completed its access page. Check the underlying connection first, before resetting the work password.

**Mentor's advice.** Identify the failure stage: network, sign-in, device check, or tunnel.

### Q58. The VPN says connected, but an internal service fails. What next?

**Answer.** I confirm the exact resource and whether one service or all internal access fails. Then I check the approved profile, test the internal name through the expected DNS path, compare another permitted internal service, and capture the error. A tunnel can be connected while routing, name resolution, availability, or permissions still block the task. If checks do not resolve it, I escalate with the results and give the user a next update time [[\[39\]]{.underline}](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/experience/connectivity-networking/connect-to-a-vpn-in-windows).

**Follow-up.** *Does being connected to the VPN grant access to every internal resource?*

No. The user still needs permissions for each resource, and network rules may limit reach. Broad access is not a workaround.

**Scenario.** The internal site resolves and shows access denied after sign-in. The service responded, so check the account and assignment rather than rebuilding the VPN tunnel.

**Mentor's advice.** A connected tunnel is one result. Verify the user\'s original task.

# Stage 4: Microsoft 365, security, and knowledge management

This stage covers Microsoft 365 applications and files, phishing and malware response, safe data handling, and knowledge base practice.

**Approach.** User reports issue → understand → assess impact → prioritize → troubleshoot → communicate → resolve or escalate → verify → document → close

**Boundary.** Use the correct account, app version, and approved guide. Protect unsynced work and sensitive information. For suspected compromise, containment through the security process can come before routine troubleshooting. Tenant-wide changes, forensics, remote wipe decisions, and policy design belong to authorized specialists.

## 4.1 Microsoft 365 applications and files

### Q59. How would you begin investigating a Microsoft 365 issue?

**Answer.** I identify the failed task, application, account, device, error, and start time. Microsoft 365 combines connected apps and cloud services, so an app problem needs separating from a wider service problem. I check whether others are affected and, if my role permits, review service health. A matching known incident means an approved workaround and a next update time. Otherwise, I continue with focused checks and record the results [[\[40\]]{.underline}](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/microsoft-365/enterprise/view-service-health?view=o365-worldwide).

**Follow-up.** *What if service health shows no active incident?*

That does not rule out a new or limited problem. I would record affected users, times, and errors, and escalate internally if the evidence suggests wider impact.

**Scenario.** Several users cannot open Teams, and a matching service advisory exists. Link their tickets to the internal incident, share the approved update, and verify recovery later. Reinstalling clients will not fix a service fault.

**Mentor's advice.** Match any service advisory to the actual symptoms.

### Q60. A Microsoft 365 app says Unlicensed Product. What would you check?

**Answer.** I confirm the app, exact message, and work or school account in use. A successful sign-in does not prove the account is licensed for that desktop app. I check license information within my access, or ask the licensing team. Connectivity and the supported activation guide come next. Open work is saved before any restart or sign-out, and I finish by confirming the user can edit [[\[41\]]{.underline}](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/microsoft-365-activation-licensing/office-install/unlicensed-product-and-activation-errors-in-office).

**Follow-up.** *Would you reset the password straight away?*

No. First I would establish whether authentication or activation failed. A password reset does not add a missing license.

**Scenario.** A new starter can use Word in the browser but cannot edit in the desktop app. Confirm the account and desktop entitlement, and offer the browser option while licensing checks the assignment.

**Mentor's advice.** Separate identity, license entitlement, and activation.

### Q61. How would you troubleshoot Outlook not sending or receiving email?

**Answer.** I confirm whether sending, receiving, or both fail, for one message or all mail, and whether the user has classic Outlook, new Outlook, or the web version. Then I check the connection state, exact error, and any delivery failure report. A web comparison separates the desktop app from the mailbox service. Depending on the evidence, I check the recipient, Outbox, quota, or service health, then send a permitted test message [[\[42\]]{.underline}](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/outlook/i-can-t-send-or-receive-messages-in-outlook).

**Follow-up.** *Does working webmail prove that desktop Outlook is damaged?*

No. It narrows the problem to the desktop app, which may have a connection, account, or configuration issue rather than corruption.

**Scenario.** Only one external recipient rejects mail, and the failure report names an invalid address. Check the address with the user through a trusted route. Do not rebuild the profile.

**Mentor's advice.** State which direction of mail flow fails, and read the error first.

### Q62. A user cannot join or hear a Teams meeting. How would you help?

**Answer.** I separate a joining problem from an audio problem. For joining, I confirm the link, account, client, and exact message, including whether the user is waiting in the lobby. For audio, I check mute status, the selected speaker and microphone, and device permissions. Another approved call or the browser version offers a comparison. For an urgent meeting, I offer an authorized alternative, then document and escalate as needed [[\[43\]]{.underline}](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/teams/meetings/i-can-t-join-a-meeting-in-microsoft-teams), [[\[44\]]{.underline}](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/teams/platform/troubleshoot-in-microsoft-teams).

**Follow-up.** *What would you check if the meeting has an echo?*

Whether nearby devices joined with microphones and speakers active. One audio connection or a headset usually helps, changed one thing at a time.

**Scenario.** A guest sees a message that the organizer must admit them. Contact the organizer and confirm the guest\'s account. Audio settings and reinstalls will not solve a lobby requirement.

**Mentor's advice.** Choose the next step by stage: admission, connection, or audio.

### Q63. How would you handle a OneDrive file that is not syncing?

**Answer.** I confirm the work account, the folder, and whether one file or all files are affected. Then I check the sync icon, error, connection, paused status, and storage limits, and compare the local file with the cloud version while preserving any newer unsynced work. The matching guide comes next, and I verify the changes appear online. The local folder is never deleted or sync reset without understanding the file state [[\[45\]]{.underline}](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/onedrive/fix-onedrive-sync-problems).

**Follow-up.** *Is a file in the OneDrive folder automatically backed up online?*

No. Its latest changes may still be waiting or may have failed to sync. The folder name alone does not prove upload succeeded.

**Scenario.** A proposal was edited while sync was paused. Preserve the newer work, find out why sync paused, and resume through the approved steps. Confirm the changes online before closing.

**Mentor's advice.** Protect the newest work first.

### Q64. A shared OneDrive or SharePoint link gives Access denied. What next?

**Answer.** I confirm the file, recipient, account, and exact error. A link can exist without granting access to everyone who receives it, so I check whether it is limited to named people or the organization, and whether access expired or was removed. The owner or authorized support team confirms the approved permission. Sharing is never broadened to clear the error. After correction, the recipient tests the original task [[\[46\]]{.underline}](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/onedrive/sharepoint/manage-sharing-and-permissions-in-onedrive-and-sharepoint).

**Follow-up.** *What if an outside partner needs the file urgently?*

I would confirm the business need and follow the external sharing approval process, or request an approved alternative. Personal storage accounts are not an option.

**Scenario.** A link for one named supplier contact was forwarded to a colleague, who cannot open it. Confirm whether they are authorized, then have the owner arrange access. Forwarding did not transfer permission.

**Mentor's advice.** Urgency does not authorize wider access.

## 4.2 Security and safe support practice

### Q65. How would you respond to a suspicious email or message?

**Answer.** I ask the user not to open links or attachments, reply, or use contact details in the message, and check whether they already interacted with it, since that changes the response. They report it through the organization\'s method, such as a phishing report button, which preserves the message. I record the time and actions, escalate suspected compromise promptly, and verify any business request through a separate trusted route [[\[47\]]{.underline}](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/security/protect-yourself-from-phishing).

**Follow-up.** *What warning signs would you look for?*

Unexpected requests, pressure to act, mismatched senders or links, and requests for credentials or payment. A familiar name does not prove safety, because accounts can be compromised.

**Scenario.** A \"payroll\" message asks the user to sign in through a new link today. Ask them to report it and verify through the known payroll route. Do not open the link to inspect it.

**Mentor's advice.** Reporting an unopened message differs from responding to exposure.

### Q66. The user clicked a suspicious link or entered credentials. What would you do?

**Answer.** I stay calm and ask exactly what happened: opening a page, running a file, entering a password, or approving an MFA prompt. The user stops interacting with it, and the event goes immediately to the security process. I record the time and details without collecting secrets, then follow approved containment and recovery steps, using a trusted device or channel where required. A password change alone may not complete the response [[\[47\]]{.underline}](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/security/protect-yourself-from-phishing), [[\[31\]]{.underline}](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/entra/identity/users/users-revoke-access).

**Follow-up.** *Why might a password reset be insufficient?*

An attacker may still hold an active session or have changed account settings. Security may need to revoke sessions and review activity.

**Scenario.** A user entered a password and approved an unexpected MFA request, and the account still works. Escalate promptly as possible compromise. Continued access is not evidence of safety.

**Mentor's advice.** Avoid blame. Accurate details and early reporting matter most.

### Q67. What would you do if a device showed signs of malware or ransomware?

**Answer.** Unusual encryption, a ransom message, or a credible security alert is a possible incident. The user stops work, I follow the approved isolation steps promptly, and I alert the security team. Isolation may mean disconnecting the network when the procedure requires it. I record the device, time, symptoms, and actions through a safe channel. Restarts, cleanup tools, and opening affected files are avoided, since they can spread damage or destroy evidence [[\[48\]]{.underline}](https://www.cisa.gov/stopransomware/ransomware-guide).

**Follow-up.** *Would you switch the computer off immediately?*

I would follow the emergency instructions. Isolation and shutdown have different effects; if isolation fails and spread continues, the procedure may require shutdown.

**Scenario.** Files are being renamed and a ransom note appears during remote support. Isolate promptly and contact security through a safe channel. Expect the remote session to drop; containment comes first.

**Mentor's advice.** Investigation and recovery belong to the authorized response team.

### Q68. How would you handle a lost or stolen work device?

**Answer.** I verify the reporter through the approved process and collect the device identifier, last known time and place, and circumstances. Security or device management is notified promptly under the lost device procedure. I record the known protection status without assuming encryption or a screen lock removes all risk. An authorized team decides on access restrictions and remote actions, and I confirm the correct device before any delegated step [[\[49\]]{.underline}](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/intune/device-management/actions/).

**Follow-up.** *Can you promise that a remote wipe has removed the data?*

No. Sending a command differs from confirmed completion. The device may be offline. I would report the actual status only.

**Scenario.** A user reports a stolen laptop, and the asset list shows their old and current devices. Escalate promptly, then verify the current asset identifier before any remote action.

**Mentor's advice.** Separate what is known, what is requested, and what the device has confirmed.

### Q69. How does least privilege apply to everyday Service Desk work?

**Answer.** Least privilege means using only the access an authorized task needs. My normal account handles routine work, and an approved elevated method is used only when necessary. Before granting delegated access to a user, I confirm the business need and approval, and a limited approved action is preferred over permanent administrator rights. I record the change and verify the task. A powerful support account is not permission to use every function [[\[2\]]{.underline}](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows-server/identity/ad-ds/plan/security-best-practices/implementing-least-privilege-administrative-models).

**Follow-up.** *What if an installer asks for administrator credentials?*

I would confirm the software is approved and use the authorized deployment or elevation route, never sharing an admin password or granting permanent rights.

**Scenario.** A user needs an approved utility for a presentation today. Use the software portal or authorized installation route and explain the timing. Endpoint protection stays on.

**Mentor's advice.** Least privilege supports authorized work while keeping access limited.

### Q70. How would you protect sensitive information during support?

**Answer.** I collect only the details needed for diagnosis and use approved ticket, remote support, and file transfer channels. Before a screen capture, the user closes unrelated confidential content. Logs and screenshots get checked for secrets and personal information, with approved redaction where appropriate. Passwords, MFA codes, and recovery secrets stay out of tickets. Accidental exposure is contained and reported through the handling procedure.

**Follow-up.** *Can you paste a ticket or log into an AI tool for help?*

Only if the organization approves that tool for that type of data. Removing a name may not remove confidential details, and any advice needs verifying.

**Scenario.** A screenshot shows an error and a customer\'s financial details. Share only the diagnostic part through an approved method. If the original is attached, follow restricted handling rather than quietly deleting it.

**Mentor's advice.** Be specific about what you collect and where it may go.

## 4.3 Knowledge management

### Q71. What makes a knowledge base article useful?

**Answer.** A useful knowledge base (KB) article helps someone recognize an issue and complete a safe task. It needs a searchable title, symptoms, affected environment, prerequisites, and clear steps with expected results, plus how to verify success and when to stop or escalate. A cause is stated only with evidence, and workarounds are labelled clearly. Before publishing, I remove sensitive information, check for duplicates, and follow the review process [[\[50\]]{.underline}](https://library.serviceinnovation.org/KCS/KCS_v6/KCS_v6_Practices_Guide/030/020).

**Follow-up.** *Must you know the root cause before recording a workaround?*

No. A tested, approved workaround helps while the cause is investigated. I would state its limits and link the incident or problem record where permitted.

**Scenario.** Resuming OneDrive sync restored a user\'s missing updates. An article could cover paused sync, confirming the account, the resume steps, and online verification, without claiming it fixes every sync error.

**Mentor's advice.** Include both the expected result and the stopping point.

### Q72. How would you use or correct an outdated KB article?

**Answer.** Before following an article, I check that it matches the symptoms, app version, device, and permissions, and I read its warnings. Only relevant approved steps are used. If a step no longer matches the screen or gives a different result, I stop and record the difference. I correct the article if my role allows, or send clear feedback to its owner, and link it to the ticket noting whether it helped [[\[51\]]{.underline}](https://library.serviceinnovation.org/KCS/KCS_v6/KCS_v6_Practices_Guide/030/030/040/020).

**Follow-up.** *Does an old review date mean the article must be discarded?*

No. The date is a reason to check, not proof it is wrong. I would validate it against the current environment and flag problems.

**Scenario.** A classic Outlook article names an option missing from new Outlook. Stop and find the matching guide, then send the owner the version mismatch instead of improvising.

**Mentor's advice.** Reusing an article includes checking it.

### Q73. How can an L1 analyst help reduce recurring incidents?

**Answer.** While handling tickets, I notice repeated symptoms and check whether cases share an application, version, location, or time. Related records get linked by process, and examples, impact, attempted fixes, and results go to the team investigating causes. Similar wording alone does not prove a common root cause. Meanwhile, approved workarounds restore service and knowledge stays current. My contribution is reliable evidence from real cases [[\[52\]]{.underline}](https://library.serviceinnovation.org/KCS/KCS_v6/KCS_v6_Practices_Guide/093).

**Follow-up.** *Would you open a problem record for every repeated ticket?*

I would follow the organization\'s criteria and my authority. A repeat justifies a look, but it is not a confirmed problem.

**Scenario.** Five users report Teams audio failures after a client update. Compare models, versions, symptoms, and times, including a working comparison. Share the pattern without claiming the update is proven responsible.

**Mentor's advice.** Separate the observed pattern from the suspected cause.

### Q74. How would you encourage self-service without dismissing the user?

**Answer.** I choose an approved article that matches the user\'s task and explain what it will help them do. Then I check they can open it, follow the steps, and have the required permissions, offering guided help or an accessible alternative if needed. Afterwards, I confirm the original task works and pass on any confusing instructions as feedback. Sending a link is one support step, not a resolution [[\[53\]]{.underline}](https://library.serviceinnovation.org/KCS/KCS_v6/KCS_v6_Practices_Guide/030/040/010/080).

**Follow-up.** *What if the user already tried the article and it failed?*

I would acknowledge the effort, ask which step failed, and use that evidence for the next check instead of repeating the article.

**Scenario.** A user cannot sign in, and the recovery article sits behind the same sign-in page. Use the accessible help route and verified recovery process, and report the barrier to the article owner.

**Mentor's advice.** Recommend the right article, stay available, and verify the outcome.

## 5.1 Introducing yourself and showing evidence

### Q75. Tell me about yourself and why you are ready for an entry-level role.

**Answer.** I am building a career in IT support, focused on helping users complete their work. Through study and practical exercises, I have learned to ask clear questions, check one possible cause at a time, and explain results simply. I am comfortable asking for guidance, and I understand workplace access comes with limits. I would bring a careful approach, useful notes, and a willingness to learn the team\'s tools [[\[1\]]{.underline}](https://www.onetonline.org/link/details/15-1232.00).

**Follow-up.** *What practical exercise could you explain in more detail?*

One I actually completed: the environment, the symptom, the checks, and how I verified the result, clearly labelled as a lab, project, placement, or paid role.

**Scenario.** The interviewer asks which ticketing system you used at work, but you have only practiced in a demo. Say so directly, then explain the fields and workflow you practiced.

### Q76. Why do you want to work in a Help Desk or Service Desk role?

**Answer.** I like understanding a practical problem and helping someone move forward. Service Desk work combines troubleshooting with communication, documentation, and follow-through. The role also includes routine requests, queue work, and escalation, and those appeal to me because I want to build sound support habits. I would judge an interaction by whether the user can complete their task and knows what happens next [[\[1\]]{.underline}](https://www.onetonline.org/link/details/15-1232.00), [[\[55\]]{.underline}](https://nationalcareers.service.gov.uk/careers-advice/interview-advice).

**Follow-up.** *Why this organization rather than any support employer?*

I would connect one verified detail from its website or job description to my preparation, such as a structured training program. No invented praise.

**Scenario.** The employer supports several external clients. Be ready to explain how you would confirm the correct client environment, contact route, and procedure before acting.

**Mentor's advice.** Show you understand the routine work as well as the parts you enjoy.

### Q77. You have no professional IT experience. What evidence can you offer?

**Answer.** I have not worked in a professional Service Desk team, so I would not claim live queue or production experience. I can show my approach through study, practical exercises, and times I helped others, explaining what I personally did, checked, and learned. I would welcome supervised work and feedback so I can build reliable habits in the real environment [[\[54\]]{.underline}](https://nationalcareers.service.gov.uk/careers-advice/interview-advice/the-star-method), [[\[55\]]{.underline}](https://nationalcareers.service.gov.uk/careers-advice/interview-advice).

**Follow-up.** *Would a customer-facing job outside IT be relevant?*

Yes, explained accurately. Listening, clarifying requests, handling frustration, and recording information transfer to support work. Technical gaps stay clearly stated.

**Scenario.** You helped at a student event with repeated registration questions. That shows communication, but it does not establish experience administering an identity system.

**Mentor's advice.** Describe an example\'s limits as clearly as its result.

### Q78. Tell me about a technical problem you solved.

**Answer.** During a college group project, a teammate could not open our shared document before a rehearsal. I asked for the exact message and checked the signed-in account. The link was meant for their college account, but the browser was using a personal one. After they switched accounts through the normal sign-in, the document opened. We finished the rehearsal, and I added an account-check reminder to our notes [[\[54\]]{.underline}](https://nationalcareers.service.gov.uk/careers-advice/interview-advice/the-star-method).

**Follow-up.** *How did you know that your action solved the problem?*

The teammate opened the document with the correct account and completed their task. That observable result is the evidence, not a claim that every sharing problem was fixed.

**Scenario.** The interviewer asks whether you changed the file permissions. In this fictional example, you did not; the existing permission worked with the right account. Do not add actions to strengthen the story.

**Mentor's advice.** Fictional example. Use your own story in STAR form.

## 5.2 Mistakes, feedback, and uncertainty

### Q79. Tell me about a mistake and how you handled it.

**Answer.** In a practice support exercise, I gave a classmate instructions for a different version of an application. When the menu did not match, I realized I had assumed the version. I acknowledged it, asked them to stop, and found the correct instructions, with no unrelated settings changed. Since then, a version check is part of my opening questions. The lesson: verify the environment first, especially when a familiar problem makes me rush [[\[54\]]{.underline}](https://nationalcareers.service.gov.uk/careers-advice/interview-advice/the-star-method).

**Follow-up.** *What if the mistake had affected a real user\'s work?*

I would report the facts promptly, check the impact, and follow the recovery and escalation process, keeping what happened separate from what I suspected.

**Scenario.** A support instruction has already caused an unexpected change. Stop further changes, preserve information, and get help. An apology matters, but a controlled response comes next.

**Mentor's advice.** Fictional example. Own the action and show a specific change in habit.

### Q80. Tell me about feedback that improved the way you worked with others.

**Answer.** During a group project, a teammate said my technical explanations were too fast. I asked which part was unclear and learned I gave several steps before they finished the first. So I switched to one step at a time, checking each result. At the next session, they completed the task without asking me to repeat anything. I learned to treat feedback as information about the other person\'s experience [[\[54\]]{.underline}](https://nationalcareers.service.gov.uk/careers-advice/interview-advice/the-star-method).

**Follow-up.** *What if you disagree with the feedback?*

I would ask for a specific example, explain my reasoning calmly, and compare against the agreed task. Safety or policy questions go to the right person.

**Scenario.** A teammate says your handover is missing the next action. Review the note together, add the owner and next step, and check it is now usable.

**Mentor's advice.** Fictional example. Show the change and its observed effect, without invented percentages.

### Q81. What would you do if you did not know the answer to a technical question?

**Answer.** I would say which part I understand and which I need to check. After clarifying the symptom, impact, and environment, I consult the approved knowledge base or vendor documentation and explain a safe next check and what it could show. If the issue exceeds my knowledge, access, or time, I escalate with evidence and keep the user informed. Guessed commands and unconfirmed causes have no place in my answer.

**Follow-up.** *What would you say if this happened during the interview?*

\"I have not used that feature yet. I would confirm the version and exact error, then check the supported guide. May I explain my troubleshooting approach?\"

**Scenario.** You recognize a command but cannot explain one of its options. Do not recommend running it. Check the official description and local procedure first.

**Mentor's advice.** Admit the specific gap, then show a useful method.

### Q82. An executive needs printing, a team cannot sign in, and malware is reported. What first?

**Answer.** I quickly establish the impact and urgency of each report and activate the security response route for the suspected malware. The wider sign-in failure goes to the incident process, so neither waits while I work on the printer. The local priority matrix and procedures guide the order. I acknowledge the executive\'s deadline, look for an approved printing alternative, and give updates. Job title alone does not set technical priority [[\[56\]]{.underline}](https://support.atlassian.com/jira-service-management-cloud/docs/how-do-i-create-a-matrix-using-impact-and-urgency-values/), [[\[48\]]{.underline}](https://www.cisa.gov/stopransomware/ransomware-guide).

**Follow-up.** *Does that mean one fixed ticket order always applies?*

No. New facts change the response. A printing issue could affect a critical service, and a sign-in report could have a simple cause. I would keep reassessing.

**Scenario.** You are the only analyst when all three reports arrive. Trigger the security and incident contacts, give each caller a next step, and request queue cover. Do not work three investigations silently.

**Mentor's advice.** Explain routing and communication as well as priority.

## 5.3 Escalation and closing the interview

### Q83. The issue needs escalation, but the user insists you fix it now. What do you say?

**Answer.** I acknowledge the disruption and explain why the next team is needed, such as restricted access or a fault beyond the L1 procedure. Then I summarize my checks and offer any permitted workaround. The handover includes impact, errors, timing, and results, and I confirm the next communication point so the ticket is not lost between teams. No repair time is promised until the receiving team confirms one [[\[2\]]{.underline}](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows-server/identity/ad-ds/plan/security-best-practices/implementing-least-privilege-administrative-models), [[\[1\]]{.underline}](https://www.onetonline.org/link/details/15-1232.00).

**Follow-up.** *What if the user says the previous analyst already tried those checks?*

I would read the notes and repeat a check only if the state changed, the evidence was incomplete, or the next team needs a current result, explaining why.

**Scenario.** The procedure ends with an administrator action, and the user offers their manager\'s password. Decline it, explain the authorized route, and escalate with the evidence.

**Mentor's advice.** Escalation is part of restoring service. Make the handover usable.

### Q84. A colleague suggests an unsafe shortcut that conflicts with the approved guide. What next?

**Answer.** I pause before any change and ask what the shortcut is meant to solve. Then I compare it with the approved procedure and explain the specific concern, such as exposing data or disabling protection. Clarification comes from the authorized support or security team, and I offer a permitted alternative where possible. I record the facts without blaming anyone. Confidence or seniority does not extend my permissions [[\[2\]]{.underline}](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows-server/identity/ad-ds/plan/security-best-practices/implementing-least-privilege-administrative-models).

**Follow-up.** *Would you always follow the written article even if it looks wrong?*

No. If a step looks unsafe or outdated, I would stop, ask for clarification, and report the exact mismatch to the owner.

**Scenario.** A colleague proposes briefly disabling endpoint protection to run an unfamiliar installer. Route it through the software request or security review process. \"Temporary\" does not make it safe.

**Mentor's advice.** Describe the risk and approval route calmly.

### Q85. What is one skill you are still improving?

**Answer.** I am improving how briefly I explain a technical issue. In practice, I sometimes give too much background before the next action. Now I prepare a short version: the symptom, the result of my check, and what happens next. A practice partner tells me what they understood and where they needed more. In a support role, I would keep seeking feedback while making sure short explanations still carry what the user needs.

**Follow-up.** *How would you show that this is improving?*

By comparing recorded practice answers or feedback over time for a clearer main point. No invented numbers, and no claim the weakness is gone.

**Scenario.** The interviewer asks for a real weakness, and you prepared \"I work too hard.\" Choose a true, manageable skill gap instead, with the practice you are doing about it.

**Mentor's advice.** Illustrative answer. Choose an honest development area and describe action.

### Q86. What questions would you ask us at the end of the interview?

**Answer.** I would ask about the work and the support available to a new analyst: which issues L1 handles most often, when escalation is expected, how training and supervised access work, and what good performance looks like in the first months. If the job description leaves something unclear, such as shift coverage, I would ask about that. I choose the most relevant questions and listen carefully [[\[55\]]{.underline}](https://nationalcareers.service.gov.uk/careers-advice/interview-advice).

**Follow-up.** *What if they already answered all your prepared questions?*

I would acknowledge that and ask one follow-up about something they mentioned, or about the next stage. No repeated questions to fill time.

**Scenario.** The interviewer says analysts support clients in several time zones. Ask which shifts this vacancy covers, how handover works, and whether on-call work is included.

**Mentor's advice.** Ask questions that help you understand this specific role.

## 5.4 Rapid-fire revision

Cover the answers. Give each distinction in one or two sentences.

[]{#tbl_rapid1 .anchor}**Table 1.** Rapid-fire fundamentals

  ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  **No.**   **Question**                                                       **Answer**
  --------- ------------------------------------------------------------------ ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  **R01**   **Incident versus service request?**                               An incident is an unplanned interruption or reduced quality. A service request asks for an agreed action, information, or access [[\[6\]]{.underline}](https://www.atlassian.com/itsm/service-request-management).

  **R02**   **Problem versus incident?**                                       A problem is a cause or potential cause of incidents. An incident can be resolved while the cause is still investigated [[\[57\]]{.underline}](https://www.atlassian.com/incident-management/devops/incident-vs-problem-management).

  **R03**   **How do impact and urgency affect priority?**                     Impact is the effect on work; urgency is how soon action is needed. Apply the organization\'s matrix, since no universal P1 to P4 mapping exists [[\[56\]]{.underline}](https://support.atlassian.com/jira-service-management-cloud/docs/how-do-i-create-a-matrix-using-impact-and-urgency-values/).

  **R04**   **Does meeting the response target mean the issue is resolved?**   No. Response and resolution are separate milestones, and an SLA may set separate targets for each [[\[58\]]{.underline}](https://support.atlassian.com/jira-service-management-cloud/docs/what-are-slas/).

  **R05**   **Is a workaround the same as removing the cause?**                No. It restores service or reduces impact while the cause remains. Record its limits [[\[57\]]{.underline}](https://www.atlassian.com/incident-management/devops/incident-vs-problem-management).

  **R06**   **What makes an escalation useful?**                               Failed task, impact, environment, error and time, checks and results, actions taken, and the help needed, with assumptions stated separately.

  **R07**   **Authentication versus authorization?**                           Authentication verifies identity. Authorization decides permitted access. Signing in does not grant access to everything [[\[59\]]{.underline}](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/entra/identity-platform/authentication-vs-authorization).

  **R08**   **Why are two passwords not MFA?**                                 MFA combines different factor types, such as something you know and something you have. Two passwords are the same type [[\[60\]]{.underline}](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/entra/identity/authentication/concept-mfa-howitworks).

  **R09**   **What does least privilege mean for L1?**                         Use only the access the authorized task needs. Available buttons do not define your permission [[\[2\]]{.underline}](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows-server/identity/ad-ds/plan/security-best-practices/implementing-least-privilege-administrative-models).

  **R10**   **What do DNS and DHCP do?**                                       DNS provides information for domain names, including addresses. DHCP supplies IP addresses and settings automatically [[\[38\]]{.underline}](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows-server/administration/windows-commands/nslookup), [[\[61\]]{.underline}](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows-server/networking/technologies/dhcp/dhcp-top).
  ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Say what each result proves, what remains unknown, and what is within L1 authority.

[]{#tbl_rapid2 .anchor}**Table 2.** Rapid-fire diagnostic judgment

  ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  **No.**   **Question**                                                            **Answer**
  --------- ----------------------------------------------------------------------- --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  **R11**   **Does a successful ping prove a website works?**                       No. Ping tests ICMP reachability, not sign-in, content, or the application service [[\[37\]]{.underline}](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows-server/administration/windows-commands/ping).

  **R12**   **Does a failed ping prove the host is down?**                          No. Filtering can block replies. Compare other approved checks [[\[37\]]{.underline}](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows-server/administration/windows-commands/ping).

  **R13**   **What does a successful nslookup tell you?**                           The queried DNS server returned a result. It does not prove the application is reachable [[\[38\]]{.underline}](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows-server/administration/windows-commands/nslookup).

  **R14**   **Why read ipconfig before changing network settings?**                 It records the current configuration. Release and renew change it and can drop a remote session [[\[36\]]{.underline}](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows-server/administration/windows-commands/ipconfig).

  **R15**   **A site opens but says Access denied. What does that suggest?**        The service responded, so check the account and permission. A network reset will not fix authorization [[\[59\]]{.underline}](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/entra/identity-platform/authentication-vs-authorization).

  **R16**   **Does a file in a OneDrive folder prove the latest edit is online?**   No. Sync may be paused or failing. Check sync state and preserve newer local work [[\[45\]]{.underline}](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/onedrive/fix-onedrive-sync-problems).

  **R17**   **Can urgency justify sharing an administrator password?**              No. Urgency affects response speed, not credential rules. Use the approved elevation route [[\[2\]]{.underline}](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows-server/identity/ad-ds/plan/security-best-practices/implementing-least-privilege-administrative-models).

  **R18**   **First response to suspected ransomware?**                             Stop routine work, isolate through the approved steps, and alert security through a safe channel [[\[48\]]{.underline}](https://www.cisa.gov/stopransomware/ransomware-guide).

  **R19**   **Example of a useful ticket note?**                                    \"10:15 local time: one user cannot open the shared file. Correct account confirmed; access denied remains. Access request sent to owner. Next contact 11:00.\"

  **R20**   **When is it appropriate to close a ticket?**                           After required verification and documentation, under the closure process. Sending a link or losing contact is not resolution.

# Glossary

Plain definitions of key terms. Employers may use different names for the same idea.

  -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  **Term**                       **Meaning**
  ------------------------------ ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  **Authentication**             Verifying who is signing in.

  **Authorization**              Deciding what an identity may access or do.

  **Change**                     Adding, modifying, or removing anything that could affect a service.

  **Conditional Access**         Sign-in requirements based on conditions such as user, app, or device.

  **DHCP**                       Automatically assigns IP addresses and network settings.

  **DNS**                        Resolves names to information such as IP addresses.

  **Escalation, functional**     Passing work to a team with the right expertise or access.

  **Escalation, hierarchical**   Raising an issue to someone with authority over a risk or blocker.

  **Impact**                     How much business activity is affected.

  **Incident**                   An unplanned interruption or reduction in service quality.

  **ITIL**                       Guidance organizations can use for IT service management.

  **ITSM**                       How an organization plans, delivers, and supports IT services.

  **KB article**                 An approved, written fix or procedure in a knowledge base.

  **L1, L2, L3**                 Support levels: first contact, further investigation, then specialist or vendor support.

  **Least privilege**            Using only the access an authorized task needs.

  **Managed device**             A device receiving organization settings, apps, and security requirements.

  **MFA**                        Multifactor authentication: identity proof using different factor types.

  **Phishing**                   A deceptive message designed to steal credentials, money, or access.

  **Priority**                   The order work is handled in, usually from impact and urgency.

  **Problem**                    The cause, or possible cause, of one or more incidents.

  **Resolved / closed**          Resolved means the fix is recorded; closed means complete under closure rules.

  **Service request**            A request for something normally provided, such as software or access.

  **SLA**                        Service level agreement: agreed targets such as response and resolution.

  **SSO**                        Single sign-on: one session giving access to several integrated apps.

  **STAR**                       Situation, task, action, result: a structure for behavioral answers.

  **Urgency**                    How soon harm becomes serious or action is needed.

  **VPN**                        Virtual private network: a protected connection to company resources.

  **Workaround**                 A way to restore work without removing the underlying cause.
  -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
